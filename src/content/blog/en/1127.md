## How to Play (Human vs. Computer)

**Board:** A six-pointed star with 121 holes. Each of the six points is a triangular camp of 10 holes; the center links all six points.

**Pieces:** You and the computer each have 10 pegs, starting in two opposite camps (all 10 holes filled). The four side camps stay empty.

**Goal:** Move all 10 of your pieces across the board and completely fill the camp opposite your starting camp.

**Moving:** On each turn, move exactly one piece. Two options, not combinable in one turn:

1. **Step:** Move one piece to an adjacent empty hole along a line.
2. **Jump:** Jump one piece over any single adjacent piece — your own or the computer's — landing in the empty hole directly beyond it. The jumped piece stays on the board; nothing is captured. The same piece may continue jumping from its new position, changing direction as needed, until no further jumps are available.

**Winning:** First side to fill all 10 holes of the opposite camp wins.

**Rules:**
- One piece moved per turn (it may make multiple jumps).
- No capturing, no removal of pieces.
- You may jump over your own pieces as well as the computer's.

**Basic tips:**
- Build "ladders" — chains of pieces that let one piece jump far in a single turn.
- Keep pieces clustered near the center line for more jump options (the computer can use the same ladders).
- Don't leave stragglers; isolated pieces must be walked one step at a time.
- Plan the endgame: the last pieces must fill specific remaining holes in the destination camp.

## History and Origins

Chinese Checkers was not invented in China and is not a form of checkers — the name was a marketing label.

The game descends from **Halma**, invented in the United States by **George Howard Monks** around 1883–1884, played on a square board with pieces racing from corner to opposite corner.

A star-shaped variant appeared soon after; in **1892** the German company **Ravensburger** patented it as **Sternhalma** ("Star Halma") — the direct ancestor of the modern game.

The star-shaped game reached the U.S. in the 1930s and became a craze. Manufacturers rebranded it with an Oriental theme and the name "Chinese Checkers" purely for commercial appeal; **Milton Bradley** patented its version under that name in **1941**. The name stuck despite the game's American and German origins.
